Honest writing for an audience of zero.

The page remembers for you

There’s a well-studied effect: writing something down encodes it in memory more deeply than reading it, hearing it, or thinking it. The reason isn’t the record — most people never re-read their journals anyway. It’s the act itself. Choosing the words forces a kind of attention that thinking alone doesn’t. When an entry fades in three days, you haven’t lost anything that mattered. The thing it was meant to do — make you notice what you noticed — has already happened.

No audience, no editor

Most people freeze when they try to journal. The page is implicitly addressed to someone: a future biographer, a future therapist, a future self mining for wisdom. That imagined reader is the inner editor wearing a costume, and it makes you write something polished or write nothing at all. A journal that erases itself in 72 hours has no audience. It isn’t even addressed to your future self. With no one to perform for, the writing turns honest by default.

Letting go on purpose

Most software treats what you make as something to hoard — archives, exports, “your data.” This one doesn’t. Some thoughts are meant to be written and then released: confessions, half-formed grievances, the small daily noise of being a person. Holding on to all of it is its own kind of weight. The fade isn’t a limitation; it’s the point. You’re not losing your thoughts. You’re choosing not to carry them.
